# Hard Conversations

An on-demand flow, same weight as `delegate-or-decline.md` and `habit-formation.md` — Jenn brings Proteus a conversation she needs to have (feedback, an accountability conversation, a conflict, a performance review, a 1:1 that's going to be hard) or one that already went badly, and it works through the flow below. The material behind this converges hard: three independent books (Supercommunicators, High Conflict, Never Split the Difference) land on the same core listening technique, which is a stronger signal than any one book alone.

**Performance reviews and 1:1s run through this flow too** — they're just a scheduled, lower-stakes version of the same thing. Crucial Accountability's diagnostic (step 2 below) and Difficult Conversations' three layers (step 1) are the actual mechanism for feedback conversations, not something separate. See also `templates/sbi.md` / `templates/starr.md` — captured wins already in that shape (from `/wrap-up`) are the raw material a real performance review draws from.

## The flow

**1. Diagnose what's actually happening.** (Difficult Conversations, Douglas Stone/Bruce Patton/Sheila Heen) Every hard conversation is three conversations at once:
- **What happened** — don't fight over who's right; explore how each person's actions fed the outcome, and separate impact from intent.
- **Feelings** — acknowledge them, on both sides, rather than suppressing them to "stay professional."
- **Identity** — the silent question underneath: am I competent, am I good, am I worthy? Performance conversations in particular are identity conversations whether anyone names it or not.

Caution while diagnosing (Talking to Strangers, Malcolm Gladwell): someone's demeanor — calm, nervous, good eye contact or none — doesn't reliably reveal what's actually true for them. The "illusion of transparency" leads people to over-read a person's visible reaction as evidence, in either direction. Diagnose from what was said and done, not from how someone looked while saying it.

**2. If this is about a broken promise or missed expectation, calibrate the severity first.** (Crucial Accountability)
- **Content** — a single instance. One conversation, addressed directly, done.
- **Pattern** — it's happened before. Name the pattern itself, not just the latest instance — "this is the third time" is a different conversation than "this happened."
- **Relationship** — repeated violations have already damaged trust. The conversation has to address the relationship before the specific instance will land at all.

**3. Voice it safely.** (Crucial Conversations, Kerry Patterson et al. — STATE) Share your facts, tell your story (your interpretation, labeled as interpretation), ask for the other person's path, talk tentatively, encourage testing of your view. If goals seem to conflict and the conversation is stuck, reset with CRIB: commit to finding mutual purpose, recognize the purpose behind their strategy even if you disagree with the strategy itself, invent a shared purpose, brainstorm new strategies together.

**4. Listen using the technique that keeps showing up independently.** Ask a question, repeat back what you heard in your own words, confirm you got it right, before responding. Supercommunicators calls this looping; High Conflict frames it as the same paraphrase-and-confirm cycle; Never Split the Difference splits it into two moves — mirroring (repeat the last few words, then stay silent) and labeling (name the emotion aloud: "it sounds like..."). Use whichever framing fits the moment; the mechanism is identical across all three, which is exactly why it's the anchor of this whole flow, not just one more technique in a list.

**5. If the words themselves need scripting**, use Nonviolent Communication's structure: a concrete Observation (fact, not judgment) → the Feeling it brings up → the underlying Need driving that feeling → a specific Request, not a demand. "When I see/hear X, I feel Y, because I need Z — would you be willing to do W?"

**6. If the other person is the hard part, not the content.** (Talking to Crazy, Dealing with People You Can't Stand) Reframe first: someone acting irrationally is protecting themselves from something, not attacking — approach as an ally trying to reach the sane person underneath, not an adversary to out-argue. If a pattern is recognizable, match the tactic to the archetype: the Tank (aggressive) needs firm boundaries stated calmly; the Sniper (covert jabs) needs the jab named out loud, in the open; the Grenade (sudden outburst) needs space before content; the Know-It-All needs questions, not corrections; the Yes Person needs specific, low-stakes commitments to actually test follow-through; the Complainer needs to be moved toward a concrete next step, not more airtime for the complaint.

**7. Giving feedback specifically — check the framing, not just the process.** (Mindset, Carol Dweck) Feedback that lands as growth-oriented praises the effort, strategy, or process ("the approach you took on X worked because...") rather than a fixed trait ("you're so good at X") — the first is repeatable and improvable, the second reads as a verdict on the person. This matters most in exactly the identity-layer moments step 1 already flags.

## What this isn't

Not a script to read verbatim, and not a cadence check — nothing here runs on a schedule. A reusable process, applied when Jenn brings a real situation to it.
