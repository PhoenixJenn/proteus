# Habit Formation

How a new habit gets set up and, if it's slipping, diagnosed — not a cadence check itself, but the mechanism two existing pieces run through: `pdr-practice-coach.md`'s Plan step (starting a new book practice) and `commands/wrap-up.md`'s Step 4a (checking on one that's already running). Also usable on-demand any time the user wants to build a habit that isn't tied to a book at all.

## Setting it up

**1. Stack it on an existing trigger, don't invent a new one.** (Habit Stacking, S.J. Scott) Attach the new habit to something that already reliably happens — ideally one of Proteus's own checkpoints (Morning Plan, Evening Reflect, the Weekly Review), since those are already trustworthy triggers. A habit with no anchor is a habit that depends on remembering it exists.

**2. Run it through the Four Laws.** (Atomic Habits, James Clear) Make it obvious, make it attractive, make it easy, make it satisfying. If any of the four is missing, that's usually why a habit that sounds good on paper doesn't survive contact with a real week — fix the weak law before adding willpower on top of it. (Breaking a bad habit instead of building one: invert all four — make it invisible, unattractive, difficult, unsatisfying.)

## Staying with it

**3. Never skip twice in a row.** (41 Self-Discipline Habits) One missed day is normal and not worth flagging. Two in a row is the actual signal — that's when Proteus should say something, not before and not only after the habit has already quietly died. Don't wait for motivation to come back before restarting, either — (The Motivation Myth, Chris Haden) motivation more often follows a small win than precedes one, so the move after a skip is just doing the next instance, not waiting to feel ready.

**4. When it's genuinely not sticking, diagnose before you re-negotiate the commitment.** (Switch, Chip & Dan Heath — the Rider, the Elephant, and the Path)
- **Rider problem** (the rational side lacks clarity): the habit itself is vague or the next action isn't concrete. Fix: make the instruction more specific, not more motivating.
- **Elephant problem** (the emotional side isn't on board): there's no real desire behind it, or it's fighting discomfort with nothing pulling the other way. Check it against Drive's three components (Daniel Pink) before guessing at a fix: is it missing **Autonomy** (it feels imposed rather than chosen), **Mastery** (no visible sense of getting better at it), or **Purpose** (it doesn't connect to anything the user actually cares about)? Fix whichever is actually missing, or shrink the habit until it's easy enough that motivation isn't load-bearing at all.
- **Path problem** (the environment is working against it): friction, timing, or surroundings make the habit harder than it needs to be, independent of willpower or clarity. Fix: change the environment, not the person — this is usually the highest-leverage fix and the one people check last.

Naming which of the three it is changes what Proteus should actually suggest — more willpower is the wrong prescription for a Path problem, and a cleaner environment doesn't fix a Rider problem.

## When the goal is getting better at something, not just doing it consistently

Everything above is about consistency — showing up to a behavior reliably. **Deep Practice** (The Talent Code, Daniel Coyle) is the complement for when the actual goal is skill growth: train at the edge of current ability, in a chunk small enough to isolate, and let mistakes happen and get corrected in the moment rather than smoothly repeating what's already comfortable. The reframe that matters here: struggle and error during practice is the signal that growth is happening, not a sign something's wrong — don't treat the frustration of deliberate practice as the same "something's not working" signal that triggers the Rider/Elephant/Path diagnosis above.

A second, independent source lands on the same mechanism from the confidence side (HBR Emotional Intelligence Series, Confidence): confidence grows from a cycle of attempting something slightly outside current ability, getting real feedback on the attempt, and incrementally improving — not from positive thinking alone. Applied: a small, specific challenge, real feedback, repeat, so confidence tracks demonstrated skill rather than outrunning it.
