# Framework Nuggets

A pre-curated "best of" from the `plan-do-reflect-www` one-pagers — short, situational frameworks Proteus can offer in a live conversation, distinct from the standing mechanisms in `daily-work-companion.md`.

**Why this exists instead of live corpus search:** the user doesn't want Proteus scanning the full one-pager corpus at runtime to "find good nuggets" — that's a token cost paid on every conversation for content that barely changes. Instead, each book gets read once, the genuinely reusable bits get distilled here, and Proteus just recalls from this short list. Grows book by book as more one-pagers get reviewed (see `pdr-practice-coach.md` for the one-pager intake flow) — this file is the running output of that process, not a separate task.

**How to use these:** offer the matching nugget when the user describes a live situation it fits — don't recite the whole list, don't force one in where it doesn't apply. One line, applied, beats a citation.

---

### Deciding something hard
**Five questions for a tough decision** (HBR's 10 Must Reads on Managing Yourself, Vol. 2 — Joseph Badaracco): What are the full consequences of each option? What are my core obligations? What will actually work in the world as it is? Who are we? What can I live with? For right-vs-right dilemmas that need judgment, not just analysis.

### Triaging a long list
**ABCDE method** (Eat That Frog!, Brian Tracy): label every task A (serious consequences if not done), B (should do, mild consequences), C (nice to do, no consequences), D (delegate), or E (eliminate) — then work strictly in order, never touching a B until every A is done. A more mechanical alternative to the Focusing Question when a list is long and needs sorting, not narrowing.

### A team's work isn't flowing
**The five thieves of time** (Making Work Visible, Dominica DeGrandis): too much work in progress, unknown dependencies, unplanned work, conflicting priorities, neglected work. Naming which thief is active is the first step to fixing a flow problem — useful when the user is describing a team/throughput issue, not a personal one.

### Declining something
**"I don't" vs. "I can't"** (The Art of Saying No, Damon Zahariades): "I can't" invites negotiation because it sounds like an obstacle to solve around; "I don't" states a standing choice. Swap the phrasing in the moment rather than over-explaining.

### "I feel busy but I don't know where the time goes"
**The 168-hour time log** (168 Hours, Laura Vanderkam): for one full week, record everything in half-hour increments, specifically (e.g. "made stir-fry," not "dinner"). At week's end, tally by category against what you assumed. A diagnostic to run occasionally, not a standing habit — most useful right before a quarterly or yearly review if the picture feels fuzzy.

### Frustrated about something outside the user's control
**Circle of Influence vs. Circle of Concern** (The 7 Habits of Highly Effective People, Stephen Covey): the Circle of Concern holds everything you worry about, most of which you can't control; the smaller Circle of Influence is what you can actually affect. Spending energy in the Concern circle shrinks the Influence circle over time; spending it inside Influence grows it. Useful alongside the strategy/goals-context check in Delegate or Decline — is this actually something to act on, or just something to stop dwelling on. When it's less "outside my control" and more "everything feels like too much at once" (The Happiness Advantage, Shawn Achor): deliberately shrink down to a small circle the user can fully master right now, then expand it step by step as confidence builds.

### Starting something new, or a goal that keeps slipping
**WOOP** (Rethinking Positive Thinking, Gabriele Oettingen): name a feasible **W**ish, vividly imagine the best **O**utcome, honestly identify the real internal **O**bstacle in the way, then make a concrete if-then **P**lan for when that obstacle shows up. The obstacle and if-then plan are what make this different from just visualizing success — pure positive fantasizing without them tends to sap the energy needed to actually pursue the goal.

### Reading a tense workplace moment
**SCARF** (Your Brain at Work, David Rock): five things that trigger a threat-or-reward response independent of the actual content of a conversation — **S**tatus, **C**ertainty, **A**utonomy, **R**elatedness, **F**airness. When a reaction seems bigger than the situation warrants, check whether one of these got threatened; often the fix is restoring that specific thing, not re-explaining the original point.

### Reframing a setback
**Perception, Action, Will** (The Obstacle Is the Way, Ryan Holiday): change how you're perceiving the obstacle first (neutral or opportunity-focused, not catastrophic), then take deliberate, persistent action to work around or through it, then apply will to endure and accept whatever genuinely can't be changed. A clean three-step frame when something's gone wrong and the instinct is to spiral rather than move.

### An underperforming or disengaged team member
**Runners, Joggers, Walkers, Riders** (Move Your Bus, Ron Clark): Runners are high-effort top performers, Joggers are steady but lack the confidence to push harder, Walkers contribute minimally and resist change, Riders are dead weight who only perform when watched. Naming which one someone actually is changes what kind of conversation helps — a Jogger needs confidence-building, a Rider needs direct accountability, and treating one as the other usually goes nowhere.

### Digging into a debate or a strongly-held opinion
**Think like a scientist, not a preacher, prosecutor, or politician** (Think Again, Adam Grant): a preacher defends sacred beliefs, a prosecutor attacks the other side's view to win, a politician tells people what they want to hear — a scientist treats their own opinion as a hypothesis, actively looks for disconfirming evidence, and updates when the evidence warrants it. Pairs well with the five-questions-for-a-tough-decision nugget above when the "decision" is really about whether to change the user's own mind. The formal version of the same move (Algorithms to Live By — **Bayesian updating**): combine what the user already believed with each new piece of evidence incrementally, rather than treating every new data point as if it arrived in a vacuum.

### Making a point land — writing or speaking
**"I believe that..." / "So what?" / "Why?"** (Get to the Point!, Brant Pinvidic — from the Communication corpus): a vague topic isn't a point. State it as a defensible assertion starting with "I believe that," then stress-test it — is it consequential enough to matter (so what), and can it actually be defended (why) — until it's sharp enough to organize a message around.

### Building a persuasive case
**Ethos, logos, pathos** (Five Stars, Carmine Gallo — Aristotle's classical framework): credibility, logical argument, emotional connection. A pitch or case that leans on only one of the three usually lands flat — check which of the three is missing before adding more of what's already there.

### Giving feedback that actually lands
**Praise the process, not the trait** (Mindset, Carol Dweck): "the approach you took on X worked because..." is repeatable and improvable; "you're so good at X" reads as a verdict on the person, not something they did. This is the framing check behind `hard-conversations.md`'s feedback step — most useful for performance reviews and 1:1s, where the identity stakes are highest.

### Restructuring a team
**Four team types + Conway's Law** (Team Topologies, Matthew Skelton/Manuel Pais): stream-aligned teams own a slice of value end-to-end, platform teams build self-service capabilities for others, enabling teams are temporary specialists who close a gap and move on, complicated-subsystem teams own something that genuinely needs deep specialist knowledge. Conway's Law says the team structure will eventually become the shape of whatever the team produces, whether anyone plans it or not — worth treating as a design input, not just an observation after the fact.

### "What am I actually supposed to be doing as a manager?"
**Purpose, People, Process** (The Making of a Manager, Julie Zhuo): a small team run well comes down to three things — the team knows what success looks like and cares about it (purpose), trusting relationships and the right staffing/coaching calls (people), and meetings/decisions that don't repeat past mistakes (process). A simple frame to sort a tangle of manager to-dos into, when it's unclear which lever actually needs pulling.

### Asking someone for something
**Frame it in terms of what they want, not what you want** (How to Win Friends & Influence People, Dale Carnegie): the only reliable way to get someone to do something is to connect it to something they already want — not to argue them into agreement. Pairs with: skip direct criticism in favor of addressing the specific behavior, and make sure any appreciation offered is genuine, not flattery deployed to get the ask through.

### Breaking into a conversation, or networking cold
**The whozit opener and grapevine glory** (How to Talk to Anyone, Leil Lowndes): approach a stranger or a new group with a low-risk, easy question ("who's that?") rather than a line that puts pressure on the exchange. Separately: a compliment said *about* someone to a third party often lands harder once it circulates back than the same compliment said directly to their face.

### Reading someone you don't know well
**Truth-default and coupling** (Talking to Strangers, Malcolm Gladwell): people are wired to assume others are honest by default — which is usually the right call, and exactly why real deception is hard to catch when it happens. Separately, behavior is often tied to specific circumstances (place, access, method) rather than being a fixed trait of the person — removing the circumstance often removes the behavior. Don't over-explain someone's actions as simply "who they are."

### Designing a milestone, onboarding, or team moment
**Elevation, Insight, Pride, Connection** (The Power of Moments, Chip & Dan Heath): a memorable moment usually has at least one of these — a boosted sensory/surprise peak, a reframing realization, recognized accomplishment, or shared meaning with others. Pair with the **peak-end rule**: people remember an experience by its most intense point and its ending, not the average of the whole thing — worth deliberately engineering both rather than leaving them to chance.

### Reading a room — daily use, in any meeting
**Baseline, then deviation** (Louder Than Words, Joe Navarro): don't look for universal body-language "tells" — learn how a specific person normally behaves, then watch for a change from *their* baseline (new tension, pulling away, a foot angled toward the exit) as the actual signal of rising discomfort or disagreement. (Pairs with — and corrects for — the illusion-of-transparency caution already in `hard-conversations.md`: this is the more rigorous version of reading someone, not a reason to stop reading them at all.) The user's own open posture, steady eye contact, and controlled gestures are read as authority before they say a word — the same mechanism, running in the other direction. Becoming Bulletproof (Evy Poumpouras, ex-Secret Service) adds the same baseline-logic from a different source: deception is more reliably spotted as an **incongruence** between words, tone, and body language than from any single tell. The same book's other useful piece: name the user's own default reaction under pressure — fight, flight, or freeze — so a specific, pre-planned alternate response can be consciously trained instead of letting the automatic one dictate the outcome.

### Every interaction — daily use
**Connect before you lead** (Leadership Presence, HBR): people judge warmth before they judge competence. Leading with warmth earns a later display of strength or competence the benefit of the doubt as confidence rather than arrogance — reversed, the same competence reads as cold or aggressive. Applies to literally any interaction, not just a specific hard moment. The actual signals that do this (The Culture Code's **belonging cues**): eye contact, physical proximity, and genuine follow-up questions — small and repeated, not one grand gesture.

### Checking the user's own communication — ongoing self-monitor, not a one-time fix
**Leaking power** (Power & Impact): over-apologizing, hedging language, and not taking credit for one's own work quietly erode influence over time, independent of the actual quality of the work. Worth a running check on emails, comments, and how the user narrates their own contributions — small, repeated habits, not a single bad moment.

### Knowing where the user's influence actually comes from
**Four power styles** (Power & Impact): formal authority, expertise, relationships, and reputation are different sources of influence, and most people lean heavily on one. A leader who relies mainly on formal authority may have little pull outside their direct chain — knowing which style is the default makes it clearer which of the other three is worth deliberately building.

### A deeper reflection than the usual review questions
**Four regret categories** (The Power of Regret, Daniel Pink): Foundation (not doing the steady work), Boldness (not taking a chance), Moral (choosing wrong over right), and Connection (letting a relationship lapse) — mapping to the needs for stability, growth, goodness, and love. Regrets of inaction outlast regrets of action over time, which is itself worth sitting with. Best run at the Yearly cadence (`daily-work-companion.md`), not daily — this is a bigger question than a routine check-in.

### Self-audit lens for how the user comes across
**Gravitas, Communication, Appearance** (Executive Presence, Sylvia Ann Hewlett): research-weighted roughly 2/3, 1/4, and a small remainder. Gravitas (composure, decisiveness, integrity under pressure) is the entry ticket; communication (voice, storytelling, reading the room) is what gets gravitas noticed at all; appearance removes a distraction but can't substitute for either. A periodic self-audit lens, not a daily check — useful alongside the quarterly personal-narrative check (`daily-work-companion.md`). Worth the caution HBR's Confidence collection adds: confidence and competence aren't the same thing, and organizations routinely mistake the appearance of gravitas for the substance behind it — a good self-audit question, not just a presentation one.

### Before committing real time or resources to something
**Premortem and failure analysis** (Think Like a Rocket Scientist, Ozan Varol): before launching, imagine the project has already failed and work backward to identify why — surfacing risks that optimism skips over while there's still time to act on them. After a real failure, extract the specific lesson rather than dwelling on it or rushing past it without one. One of the strongest tools in this whole batch — a genuine before/after pair, not just a before-the-fact check.

### Deciding what to tackle first on something new and uncertain
**The monkey and the pedestal** (Think Like a Rocket Scientist, Ozan Varol — added by the user, not in the original site summary when first reviewed): if the actual goal is a monkey reciting Shakespeare on a pedestal, build the monkey first. The pedestal is fast, visible, reassuring progress that proves nothing about whether the hard part is even possible; the monkey is the part most likely to kill the project, so tackling it first is what reveals, as early as possible, whether the whole thing can work at all. The direct check against planning something new: is the "pedestal" (the easy, visible work) getting done first just because it feels like progress, while the real "monkey" (the part that could actually fail) sits untouched?

### Stuck on the first framing of a problem
**Reframe before you solve** (What's Your Problem, Thomas Wedell-Wedellsborg / Think Like a Rocket Scientist, Ozan Varol / HBR Guide to Critical Thinking — three independent sources converge here): the way a problem is first presented usually isn't the only way to frame it, and most wasted effort goes into solving the wrong framing. Reframe by asking what's being left out, who has a stake in it, where exceptions happen, and whether the real issue is adjacent to the stated one — the classic example: a building's "slow elevator" complaint got fixed with lobby mirrors, because the actual problem was that the wait felt boring, not that the elevator was slow. The HBR Guide's version of the same move: deliberately examine the situation from multiple stakeholders' viewpoints, and zoom out to the bigger picture before zooming back into the details.

### Engaging with the unknown before committing
**Embrace uncertainty, use thought experiments** (Think Like a Rocket Scientist, Ozan Varol): treat ambiguity as necessary raw material for a breakthrough, not something to eliminate before acting — certainty often isn't available up front, and waiting for it is its own decision. Before committing real resources, run an Einstein-style thought experiment: a specific, imagined scenario that stress-tests the idea cheaply. Pairs with the five-questions-for-a-tough-decision nugget above when the uncertainty is about a decision, not just an idea.

### Breaking from "how it's always been done"
**First-principles thinking** (Think Like a Rocket Scientist, Ozan Varol): instead of reasoning by analogy (copying what's worked before) or deferring to expert consensus, break the problem down to its most basic, verifiable truths and rebuild a solution from there. Expertise itself can become a trap — deep familiarity with one way of doing things can blind someone to a fresh approach, which is why some breakthroughs come from outsiders who haven't been grooved into the standard method.

### A daily habit for staying creatively sharp
**Collecting dots** (What's Next Is Now, Jeremy Pferdt): small, intentional daily choices — a different route, a conversation with someone outside the usual circle — gathered as raw material, then actively connected into novel ideas later. Curiosity and openness reframed as a trainable daily habit, not a fixed trait.

### Looking for a breakthrough combination
**Seek the intersection** (The Medici Effect, Frans Johansson / The Innovator's DNA, Jeff Dyer et al.): breakthrough ideas disproportionately happen where different fields, cultures, or disciplines collide — the brain defaults to familiar, within-field associations, which is exactly what blocks the non-obvious connection. Associational Thinking (the hub skill behind disruptive innovators) is fed by deliberately networking outside the usual industry/peer group, not by thinking harder within it.

### Redesigning the environment instead of relying on willpower
**Constraints produce creativity** (Inside the Box, Drew Boyd/Jacob Goldenberg): deliberately imposed constraints — of time, materials, budget, or choice — reliably produce better outcomes than unlimited freedom, because removing the easy default option forces real experimentation. Same mechanism as Switch's Path-problem fix in `habit-formation.md`: redesign the environment so the desired behavior becomes the easy one, rather than asking for more discipline.

### Running a group brainstorm
**IDEO's ground rules** (The Art of Innovation, Tom Kelley): sharpen the focus to a specific, customer-centered problem statement; post playful rules (defer judgment, encourage wild ideas); number ideas as they're generated to track volume; build on others' ideas rather than only adding new ones; keep the whole idea space visible on a shared surface; warm up with something unrelated before the real problem.

### Finding process waste on a team
**"What stupid rule would you kill?"** (Kill the Company, Lisa Bodell): a short, independent-first exercise — everyone writes down the one rule, report, or approval step that gets in the way of real work. Remove one as a test; if nothing breaks, the removal becomes permanent. Low-cost, concrete, and testable rather than a vague call for "less bureaucracy."

### Finding the next buildable idea
**The adjacent possible** (Where Good Ideas Come From, Steven Johnson): breakthrough innovation usually combines existing elements just past the current boundary of what's known or available, not a leap far beyond it. An idea can be correct and still be unbuildable if the surrounding technology/context hasn't caught up yet — worth checking whether an idea is actually one step out, or several.

### Strategy/innovation work specifically
**Jobs to be done, and sustaining vs. disruptive innovation** (The Innovator's Solution / The Innovator's Dilemma, Clayton Christensen): segment by the specific job a customer is trying to get done in context, not by demographics — the famous example being milkshakes bought by commuters as a one-handed, long-lasting companion for a boring drive, not a dessert. Separately: sustaining innovations improve what existing best customers already value (incumbents are naturally good at these); disruptive innovations start simpler, cheaper, and worse by mainstream standards, win over an overlooked segment first, then improve until they take the mainstream market too — by which point the incumbent's own good management, rationally serving its existing customers, is what blinded it.

**MVP and build-measure-learn** (The Lean Startup, Eric Ries): ship the simplest version that allows one full turn of the loop, measure real behavior rather than opinions, then decide to persist or pivot. Speed of learning is the goal, not speed of building. Test under real, whole-system conditions, not isolated components (Think Like a Rocket Scientist) — an idealized test can miss the exact failure mode that would show up in the real one.

**Explore/exploit ambidexterity** (Lead and Disrupt, O'Reilly/Tushman): the discipline that makes a core business successful actively blocks the exploration needed to build what's next — the fix is running both simultaneously, not sequentially. Most innovation programs only build one or two of three needed capabilities — ideation, incubation (protected teams testing against real customers), and scaling (integrating a proven venture without killing what made it work) — which is why splashy idea contests quietly stall with no path to scale a winner.

**Just Cause as a north star** (The Infinite Game, Simon Sinek): a vision worth sacrificing for — tested against being for something, inclusive, service-oriented, resilient, and idealistic — functions as the thing every strategic decision gets measured against, replacing short-term metrics as the primary compass. Cross-references the strategy/goals-context check already in `delegate-or-decline.md`.

### Making a message stick — daily use, writing or presenting
**SUCCES** (Made to Stick, Chip & Dan Heath): six traits of a message that's actually remembered and acted on — **S**imple (lead with the single core message), **U**nexpected (break the pattern to earn attention), **C**oncrete (sensory, specific language, not abstraction), **C**redible (a vivid detail or one powerful example beats statistics alone), **E**motional (connect to what people already care about), **S**tories (wrap it in a narrative people can mentally rehearse acting on). Run a draft through all six and see which are missing — that's usually the actual fix, not a rewrite from scratch. The Heart of Change's "show, don't tell" (424 different glove types piled on a boardroom table, instead of a memo about wasteful purchasing) is the Concrete trait in action: vivid, physical evidence moves people faster than a written analysis does.

### Leading an organizational change effort
**Kotter's eight steps** (Leading Change, John Kotter): create urgency, build a guiding coalition, form a strategic vision, enlist a volunteer army, remove barriers, generate short-term wins, sustain acceleration, institute the change into culture — in sequence, since each step depends on the one before it. Skipping steps or declaring victory too early is the most common reason change efforts stall. The "short-term wins" step is the same mechanism already running in `/wrap-up`'s monthly keeper-wins and the Power of Moments nugget above — visible early proof that something is working, not just a motivational nicety.

### Why a well-run organization can't see a real shift coming
**Dual operating system** (That's Not How We Do It Here!, Kotter/Rathgeber): a disciplined management hierarchy keeps daily operations efficient and reliable; a separate, informal, volunteer-driven leadership network is what senses change and drives new ideas. The same standardized practices that make an org efficient in stable conditions are often exactly what blind it to a real threat — most organizations default to one strength (well-managed but unable to innovate, or full of ideas but operationally chaotic) and rarely build both at once.

### Stuck waiting to feel ready for a personal change
**Movement precedes clarity** (Who Moved My Cheese?, Spencer Johnson): taking action into the uncertainty reduces fear faster than waiting until the fear subsides does — the same finding as the "don't wait for motivation" line already in `habit-formation.md`, from a different angle (adapting to an external change, not building a habit). Pairs with "smell the cheese often": periodically check whether the current situation is still actually working, instead of waiting for it to visibly break before responding.

### Recognizing the user's own mentoring/management style
**Three superboss styles** (Superbosses, Sydney Finkelstein): Iconoclasts mentor almost by osmosis, drawing talent into their creative orbit; intensely competitive leaders push people past what they think they can do; Nurturers deliberately and supportively develop careers. The point isn't to pick a style to copy — it's recognizing which one already fits the user's own temperament, rather than forcing a style that isn't natural.

### What's actually driving satisfaction — the user's own or someone they manage
**Hygiene factors vs. motivators** (How Will You Measure Your Life, Clayton Christensen, drawing on Herzberg): salary, status, and working conditions are hygiene factors — their absence causes dissatisfaction, but more of them doesn't create satisfaction past a point. Meaningful work, growth, and recognition are the actual motivators. Complements Drive's Autonomy/Mastery/Purpose, already folded into `habit-formation.md`'s Elephant-problem diagnosis — a different cut on the same territory.

### Finding out what the user actually prioritizes, not what they say they do
**Resource allocation is the real strategy** (How Will You Measure Your Life, Clayton Christensen): a person's true priorities aren't what they claim to value, they're where time, energy, and money actually go day to day — the same logic a company's real strategy is revealed by thousands of small resourcing decisions, not its official plan. Pairs directly with the 168-hour time log nugget above: that's the instrument for actually measuring where this is true or false, rather than guessing.

### Working with a team that's mostly remote or async
**Digital Body Language's four laws** (Erica Dhawan): Value Visibly (show appreciation explicitly — it can't be read off body language over text), Communicate Carefully (over-clarify to remove ambiguity tone used to resolve in person), Collaborate Confidently (build psychological safety deliberately into virtual teamwork), Trust Totally (the outcome of getting the first three right). Two sharp specifics: response time functions as a stand-in for respect even when the delay was innocent, and the choice of channel itself signals how urgent or important a message is — picking the wrong one misrepresents the message regardless of its content.

### A blunt gut-check on fit or retention
**"Would I fight to keep them?"** (Powerful, Patty McCord): for each person on the team, ask honestly whether the user would fight to keep them if they said they were leaving for a similar role elsewhere. A "no" isn't a verdict on the person — it's information that the current role isn't the right fit, worth surfacing directly rather than carrying quietly into every future 1:1.

### Building an actually inclusive team or process
**Redesign the system, not the intentions** (Actions Speak Louder / Beyond Diversity / Inclusion — three books converge on this): default recruitment, interview, and retention processes reproduce whoever already succeeds in them, regardless of stated values or a mission statement. Actions Speak Louder's countermeasure — structured, standardized interview questions in place of open-ended ones that reward candidates who resemble existing employees — is the most concrete, immediately usable piece. The underlying move is the same one as a premortem: find where the default process is quietly working against the stated goal, before assuming good intentions will be enough.

### Choosing when to stop searching and commit
**The 37% rule** (Algorithms to Live By, Brian Christian/Tom Griffiths): when choosing sequentially among options that can't be revisited once passed on — hiring, apartment hunting, how long to keep evaluating before committing — spend the first 37% of the expected search purely gathering information without committing, then choose the next option that beats everything seen in that phase. Drawn from the mathematics of the secretary problem; it maximizes the odds of landing the actual best option under those constraints, not just a good-enough one.

### Clearing out stale commitments
**Caching, least recently used** (Algorithms to Live By): keep the most-used items or information closest at hand, and periodically clear out what hasn't been touched in a long time. The computing version of what the existing Weekly Review (`daily-work-companion.md`) already does by hand — a stale, untouched commitment is exactly what that pass should be finding.

### Stuck on a problem that feels impossible
**Relaxation** (Algorithms to Live By): temporarily drop one hard constraint, solve the easier version of the problem that remains, then use that solution as a starting point for the real one. A different move than the reframe-before-you-solve nugget above — this doesn't change how the problem is framed, it removes a piece of it on purpose to get unstuck, then puts the piece back.

### Before finalizing a real decision
**The 4-step check** (The Art of Strategic Decision-Making): Widen the options beyond the first ones that come to mind, Reality-test the assumptions against outside evidence, Attain distance before deciding (don't decide in the heat of the moment), and Prepare to be wrong by naming in advance what would signal the decision is failing. That last step is a lighter, ongoing cousin of the premortem nugget above — a standing tripwire instead of a one-time pre-launch exercise.

### Working through a decision alone or with a group
**Six Thinking Hats**: deliberately rotate through six perspectives before settling — facts, emotion, caution, optimism, creativity, and process — rather than defaulting to whichever one comes most naturally. Useful specifically when a decision keeps getting stuck in just one or two of these modes (all caution, or all optimism) without anyone noticing.

### Protecting decision quality across a long day
**Decision fatigue**: repeated decision-making depletes the quality of later choices, making them more impulsive or lower-quality regardless of their actual importance. Reinforces why Deep Work's protected block and anything that actually matters belongs early in the day, not squeezed in after everything else.

### Evaluating a success story or "what worked for them" advice
**Survivorship bias, the actionable version** (The Art of Thinking Clearly, Rolf Dobelli): before adopting a winner's habits or strategy, deliberately go looking for people who did the same thing and failed — failures are invisible in most success stories by default. Only if their approach looks meaningfully different from the winner's does the strategy look causally credible rather than just lucky. Worth checking alongside two of the book's other common traps: the **sunk cost fallacy** (continuing something because of what's already invested, not its future value) and **loss aversion** (losses feel roughly twice as painful as equivalent gains feel good, which quietly skews everyday risk-taking toward excess caution). A fourth, from the HBR Guide to Critical Thinking: **anchoring** — an early number or impression (a first offer, a first estimate) quietly pulls every subsequent judgment toward it, even once it's known to be arbitrary.

### Committing to a timeline or estimate
**The Outside View** (HBR Guide to Critical Thinking, tracing to Kahneman and Tversky's planning fallacy): instead of reasoning only from the specifics in front of the user, check how comparable situations actually played out elsewhere — review how similar past projects actually finished, rather than trusting their own gut estimate. The base rate is usually more accurate than the inside view feels like it should be.

### Something feels urgent
**Reflective urgency**: distinguish real time pressure from perceived urgency, and build in a deliberate pause before reacting to something that feels like it can't wait. Most "urgent" isn't, once named as a feeling rather than a fact.

### Comparing multiple real options
**Decision architecture**: set the evaluation criteria *before* looking at the specific options, not after — comparisons quietly bend toward whichever option is already favored once the criteria get picked to fit it. Pairs with the existing 4-step check and Five Questions nuggets above; this is the step that keeps either of them honest.

### The map of EQ — where the rest of this library already lives
**Four EQ skills** (Emotional Intelligence 2.0, Travis Bradberry): Self-Awareness, Self-Management, Social Awareness, Relationship Management, in that order — each builds on the one before it. A lot of what's already in this library sorts cleanly into these: Self-Awareness is the diagnosis steps throughout `hard-conversations.md` and `habit-formation.md`; Self-Management is redirection, not suppression — noticing an impulsive reaction and consciously steering it toward the longer-term goal, not just clamping down on it; Social Awareness is baseline-then-deviation, SCARF, and belonging cues, already in this file; Relationship Management is essentially `one-on-ones.md` and `hard-conversations.md` in full. Mostly useful as a map for noticing which quadrant a live situation is actually in, not a new technique on its own.

### Separating the emotional case from the logical one
**Emotion vs. reason list** (Emotional Intelligence 2.0): when a decision feels emotionally charged, write the emotional argument and the logical argument side by side, as two separate columns. The act of separating them, not just naming them, is what clarifies the judgment — a charged decision usually feels like one tangled argument until it's actually split in two.

### Building EQ as a daily habit, not a one-time insight
**Three EQ journals** (Emotional Intelligence Habits, Travis Bradberry): an emotion journal (trigger, feeling, reaction), an impulse journal (impulsive decisions and their triggers, to practice delaying gratification), and a social journal (interactions and the nonverbal cues noticed in others). A candidate habit to stack onto the existing Evening Reflect checkpoint (`daily-work-companion.md`) via `habit-formation.md`'s setup process, if the user wants to adopt one — not a new standing step on its own. Sleep (regular wake times, less afternoon caffeine, less blue light before bed) is treated in the same book as a prerequisite for emotional regulation, not a separate wellness topic — worth knowing even without turning it into a tracked habit.

### Calibrating whether the user is actually under- or overconfident
**Imposter syndrome and the beginner's bubble, paired** (HBR Emotional Intelligence Series, Confidence): imposter syndrome — persistent self-doubt despite real accomplishment — is near-universal among accomplished professionals, worth normalizing rather than treating as a personal flaw. Its mirror image, the beginner's bubble, is just as real: novices sometimes rate their own performance far higher than it actually is, and that early overconfidence can block real learning. The two aren't opposites to average out — the useful move is figuring out honestly which one is actually happening in a given moment, since the fix is different for each.

### Before a high-stakes moment
**Mental preparation** (HBR Emotional Intelligence Series, Confidence): visualization, controlled breathing, positive self-talk, and a pre-performance ritual, borrowed from athletes and transferable to a big presentation or hard conversation. Legitimate, research-backed version of the one piece of The Silva Mind Control Method for Business Managers that was worth keeping when that book was otherwise skipped for its ESP framing.

### Making a request or a case land
**Cialdini's six (now seven) levers of influence** (Influence and Persuasion / Influence: The Psychology of Persuasion — Robert Cialdini): reciprocity, social proof, authority, consistency, liking, and scarcity. Most effective combined deliberately rather than leaned on one at a time — pairing a small favor with visible evidence that respected peers already agree, for instance, rather than relying on either lever alone. The canonical, most-cited framework in this whole territory; pairs with the existing "frame it in terms of what they want" (Carnegie) and ethos/logos/pathos (Five Stars) nuggets. A cluster of related books add specific tactics worth folding in rather than treating as separate frameworks:
- **Door-in-the-face** (Influence): open with a large request expected to be refused, then follow with the smaller request actually wanted — the refusal of the first makes the second feel like a fair concession.
- **Unity, a seventh principle** (Pre-Suasion, Cialdini's own later addition): shared identity is an especially strong driver of cooperation, beyond the original six.
- **Contrast and Involvement** (Maximum Influence — the two genuinely new pieces from a book that otherwise just renames the same six): a choice or price judged right after a more extreme comparison point reads very differently than in isolation; active participation invests people in an outcome more than passively receiving information does.
- **"Because" plus any reason** (Magic Words, the classic Ellen Langer copier-line study): people respond to the presence of a justification more than its actual logical strength — adding "because..." before a request substantially increases compliance even when the reason given is weak.
- **Pre-suasion itself**: the moment right before a request shapes its success more than the content of the request does — whatever holds someone's attention right before a decision gets treated as disproportionately relevant to that decision, so deliberately directing attention beforehand (asking about a time they were generous, right before a donation ask) primes receptiveness.

**Ethical guardrail, worth stating explicitly given how much tactical material is in this nugget:** legitimate pre-suasion and influence highlight genuinely true, relevant features of a real offer — the line into manipulation is deception, not influence itself. Same reasoning that kept Flip the Script and Pitch Anything (both from the Communication corpus) out of this library entirely.

### Getting someone else — a report, a team — to actually change a behavior
**The six-source grid** (Influencer, Patterson/Grenny/et al.): first narrow to the 2-3 **vital behaviors** that would actually drive the result, out of dozens of possible actions — spreading effort across everything dilutes all of it. Then, for each vital behavior, check it against two drivers (motivation, ability) across three domains (personal, social, structural) — a six-cell grid — and design at least three or four of the six sources into the plan, not just one lever like a memo or an incentive. Personal motivation means connecting the behavior to something intrinsically meaningful, not arguing people into caring; structural ability means redesigning the environment so the behavior becomes the path of least resistance — the same move as `habit-formation.md`'s Path-problem fix, applied to someone else's habit instead of the user's own.

### Unlocking motivation someone already has but hasn't said out loud
**The readiness-scale questions** (Instant Influence, Michael Pantalon — grounded in motivational interviewing): on a scale of 1-10, how ready are you to do X; why didn't you pick a lower number; imagine you did it — what would the positive outcomes be; why do those outcomes matter to you personally; what's the next step. Takes about seven minutes. The pivot is the second question — asking why *not* a lower number has the person defend the change itself, rather than defending the status quo against someone else's argument for it. Even a 1 or 2 on the scale is never a true zero; the method finds and amplifies whatever spark already exists instead of manufacturing motivation from nothing. Useful inside a 1:1 (`one-on-ones.md`) when someone's stuck, not as a replacement for the structure already there. The broader skillset this specific script is drawn from (How to Do Motivational Interviewing — **OARS**): Open-ended questions, Affirmations, Reflective listening, and Summarizing. Reflective listening in particular means restating what the person said, sometimes surfacing a deeper meaning underneath it, rather than debating or advising — the same mechanism as the mirror/loop listening technique already anchoring `hard-conversations.md`, applied specifically to drawing out someone's own reasons rather than resolving a conflict.

### Prepping for — or running — an interview
**Four interview styles** (Interviewology, Anna Papalia): Charmers lead with likability but can underdiscuss their actual qualifications; Challengers assert value by pushing back and questioning, risking a read of dominance; Examiners default to structure and rules, which can land as rigid; Harmonizers mirror the interviewer and become deferential, which can read as passive. Everyone has a default style that shows up under pressure — not just in interviews, but in performance reviews and negotiations too — and naming it (the user's own, and the interviewer's) is more useful than memorizing scripted answers. Likability and competence are separate signals; winning warmth at the expense of demonstrating qualification is a common, specific failure mode worth checking for directly.

### The relationship nothing else in this system covers
**Managing up, four moves** (Managing Up — reinforced by a second independent source, The Manager's Path, which devotes a full chapter to the same relationship): understand your manager's priorities and, further up, what *they're* measured on and pressured by — a manager who looks good to their own boss has more capital to advocate for the team. Set the relationship's tone deliberately in the first weeks rather than letting it form by default. Match the format and frequency of updates to the manager's preference, not the user's own. Trust compounds incrementally through small, consistent follow-through, not one big gesture. Everything else built so far in this corpus — `one-on-ones.md`, `hard-conversations.md`, the delegation lens — is written from the perspective of managing people below the user; this is the only piece covering the direction above them.

### Finding a career direction that's actually energizing, not just plausible
**Super strengths** (The Squiggly Career, Helen Tupper/Sarah Ellis): the specific overlap between what the user is good at and what actually energizes them — not skill alone, which is the same gap the Happiness Matrix fold-in above (`delegate-or-decline.md`) checks for at the task level. Pairs with Drive's Autonomy/Mastery/Purpose (`habit-formation.md`) and the hygiene-vs-motivators nugget: three independent lenses on the same underlying question, worth triangulating rather than picking just one. The Diary of a CEO's **unfair advantage** law adds a fourth: identify and double down on whatever asymmetric edge the user already holds, rather than competing on effort alone — grit without a real edge is just working harder at the same odds.

### A transition up the management ladder
**The management ladder + skip-levels** (The Manager's Path, Camille Fournier): mentor → tech lead → managing one team → managing managers → VP/exec scope — each rung requires genuinely new skills that don't automatically transfer from the one before it. A tech lead still does the work but now owns direction; a manager of managers stops solving problems directly and has to judge whether a team is healthy without doing the work themselves. At that level, skip-level meetings are the diagnostic — a structured way to sense-check whether a directly-reporting manager's account of their team matches what the team itself actually feels.

### Stepping into a new leadership situation
**STARS** (originally The First 90 Days, Michael Watkins; Master Your Next Move is his own later extension of it): Start-up, Turnaround, Accelerated-growth, Realignment, Sustaining-success — the right leadership style depends heavily on which situation the user is actually stepping into, independent of anyone's individual task-relevant maturity (`one-on-ones.md`). The same leader should be far more directive in a turnaround than in a role that's already working well; misreading which STARS situation it is, is a common derailment cause. The original pairs STARS with the **5 Ps** — products, processes, projects, plants/facilities, and people — as the dimensions to diagnose it across, since a single team can genuinely be a turnaround in one dimension and sustaining success in another; treating the whole situation as one STARS label misses that. One scenario gets its own explicit move: being promoted over former peers needs an early, direct conversation that resets the relationship rather than letting the old dynamic quietly persist.

### Getting things done without formal authority over the people involved
**Borrowed authority** (from the Chief of Staff literature, generalized beyond that specific role): when a role requires driving outcomes across teams that don't report to you, the only real lever is relationships and delegated trust, not org-chart position. Worth naming explicitly in any cross-functional situation, not just a literal Chief of Staff one — it reframes "I have no authority here" from a blocker into the actual operating mode.

### Negotiating a starting number
**Take the higher salary now** (How to Become CEO, Jeffrey Fox): future raises are usually set as a percentage of current pay, so negotiating hard on a starting salary has outsized long-term leverage compared to the same effort spent negotiating a later raise.

### Chronic firefighting that never actually gets fixed
**The Three Ways** (The Phoenix Project, Gene Kim et al.): Flow — optimize the performance of the whole system, never a single silo, and stop defects moving downstream. Feedback — build fast, amplified loops running back from operations to development, not just forward. Continual Learning — build a culture that rewards calculated risk-taking and treats failure as a source of mastery, not something to hide. The overarching philosophy underneath Making Work Visible's five-thieves diagnostic already in this file — this is the sequence, that's the symptom list.

### An organization keeps overloading the same person
**Find your Brent, and the full Theory of Constraints cycle** (The Phoenix Project's applied version; the original formal method is The Goal, Eliyahu Goldratt): every overloaded team has a single irreplaceable person or team who's become an undocumented bottleneck — find them and protect/offload their load first, before anything else. Theory of Constraints applied to people specifically, sharper than the general 80% utilization check already in `delegate-or-decline.md`: that flow checks *the user's own* capacity; this is the diagnostic for spotting whether someone *else* has quietly become the whole system's single point of failure. The Goal's original five-step cycle, worth the full version, not just the "find them" headline: identify the system's constraint, decide how to exploit it without spending capital, subordinate every other decision to that exploitation decision, elevate the constraint by investing in capacity only if still needed, then return to step one — the next bottleneck has to be found again, since resolving one constraint just reveals the next. Local efficiency can actively hurt the whole system: optimizing a non-constraint resource to 100% utilization just builds excess inventory elsewhere, the same point The Phoenix Project's First Way makes about flow. One more angle worth checking before looking elsewhere (The 7 Habits of Highly Successful Leaders, Vinay Nawaz): sometimes the constraint is the user themselves — identify where their own direct involvement has quietly become the bottleneck a team depends on, then redesign the workflow and decision rights so fewer things actually require them.

### What actually deserves to be the goal
**Improvement of daily work, as its own goal** (The Unicorn Project's Five Ideals, Gene Kim): treating *how* the work gets done as more important than the work itself — worth noticing this is close to the premise this whole Proteus project runs on, not just a line from a novel about IT.

### Leadership character, in aphorisms
**Colin Powell's rules** (It Worked for Me): perpetual optimism is a force multiplier — visible, communicated optimism from whoever's leading changes how hard a team fights through a setback, independent of whether the optimism is fully warranted yet. Own your call, publicly — take sole responsibility for a decision that went badly rather than distributing blame across the chain, even when distributing it would be easier. Detail work is leadership work — mundane attentiveness to logistics and small facts is what lets faster, better high-level judgment calls get made; it's not something to delegate away entirely just because the role has grown past it.

### Diagnosing whether something is actually a strategy
**The kernel, and the four hallmarks of bad strategy** (Good Strategy/Bad Strategy, Richard Rumelt): a real strategy has three parts — a diagnosis that simplifies a complex situation down to the one or two factors actually determining the outcome (roughly 80% of the work is here), a guiding policy for dealing with that obstacle, and coherent, mutually reinforcing actions that carry it out, not a wish list of disconnected initiatives. Most things called "strategy" fail one of four hallmarks: fluff, failing to actually face the challenge, mistaking a goal for a strategy, or bad objectives — either too vague to act on or just an unprioritized laundry list. Good strategy concentrates limited resources at a single pivot point rather than spreading attention evenly.

### The other canonical modern strategy framework
**The five-question cascade** (Playing to Win, A.G. Lafley/Roger Martin): what is the winning aspiration; where will we play; how will we win in that arena; what capabilities have to be in place to win that way; what management systems are needed to build and sustain those capabilities. Each answer constrains the next, and the authors insist the choices have to be made together — a where-to-play choice made without a clear how-to-win is just a target list, not a strategy. Complements Rumelt's kernel above rather than competing with it: Rumelt diagnoses what's actually in the way, Lafley/Martin cascades the choices once the obstacle is understood.

### Strategy vs. just doing things better
**Operational effectiveness is not strategy** (Michael Porter, via HBR's 10 Must Reads on Strategy): doing existing activities better than competitors is copyable and eventually gets copied; real strategy means choosing a distinct, defensible position and making explicit trade-offs — deliberately choosing what *not* to do. Competitive advantage compounds when a set of activities reinforce each other, which is why a rival can copy individual pieces and still fail to replicate the whole system.

### Finding differentiation instead of competing head-on
**The four actions grid** (Blue Ocean Strategy, Kim/Mauborgne): for any set of factors competitors are judged on, ask which should be eliminated, reduced, raised, or created. Answering all four at once is what breaks the usual trade-off between low cost and differentiation, rather than picking a spot on the existing spectrum between them.

### Thinking several moves ahead in a negotiation or competitive situation
**Game theory essentials** (The Art of Strategy, Dixit/Nalebuff): a Nash equilibrium is a set of choices where no player can improve their own outcome by unilaterally switching; a dominant strategy is stronger still — best regardless of what anyone else does. The prisoner's dilemma's real lesson: individually rational choices can still produce a collectively worse outcome for everyone involved. A threat or promise only changes behavior if it's credible — deliberately limiting your own future options is what makes a commitment believable rather than just stated.

### Strategic thinking as a personal skill, not just a strategy-formulation method
**The Six Disciplines** (Michael Watkins): three "seeing" disciplines — pattern recognition under uncertainty, systems analysis (deliberately simplified models of a complex reality), and mental agility (shifting altitude and anticipating others' moves) — and three "mobilizing" disciplines — structured problem-solving as a team process, visioning through powerful simplification, and political savvy, treated as a legitimate leadership skill rather than something cynical. Pairs with the Innovator's DNA discovery skills already in this file: one is about spotting opportunity, this is about executing on what's already been spotted.

### Staying clear-headed in a high-stakes moment
**Dual awareness** (Deliberate Calm, Jacinta Jiménez/Aiko Bethea/David Bradford): simultaneously track two things at once — the external situation and one's own internal state, the emotions and assumptions driving the reaction. Most people default into a fixed, habitual reaction under stress; the second channel of awareness is what creates an actual pause between trigger and response, opening space for a deliberate choice instead of a reflexive one. A more active mechanism than just "notice the urge" — it's tracking two channels, not one.

### Diagnosing what level the user is actually leading at
**Five Levels of Leadership** (John Maxwell): Position — people follow because they have to; Permission — because they want to; Production — because of the results the leader delivers; People Development — because of what the leader has done to grow them; Pinnacle — out of respect, built over years. Worth diagnosing which level is actually in play with a specific person or team, rather than assuming title alone is doing the work.

### Taking total responsibility, and staying level-headed under a pile of problems
**Extreme Ownership's four principles** (Jocko Willink/Leif Babin): Cover and Move — teams support each other toward one shared goal instead of competing in silos; Keep It Simple — plans and communication stripped down so they still work when things go wrong; Prioritize and Execute — in a crisis, identify the single highest-priority problem, solve it, then move to the next, rather than splitting attention across all of them at once; Decentralized Command — empowered small teams who understand the mission's intent well enough to decide without waiting for approval. The underlying premise sharper than anything else in this library on the topic: total ownership of every outcome, including failures caused by people below the leader, is what actually drives a team to fix problems rather than defend against blame. The companion move for when several problems hit at once (Leadership Strategy and Tactics, same authors): physically or mentally **detach** from the tactical noise first, rank what's actually highest-priority from that wider view, then re-engage with just the one thing — getting pulled into the loudest problem while a bigger one goes unaddressed is the actual failure mode this guards against.

### Getting to the real reason behind a symptom
**The 5 Whys** (Good Leaders Ask Great Questions, John Maxwell): ask why in response to each answer, roughly five times in a row, to move from a surface-level symptom to the actual underlying reasoning or motivation — applied either to a team/process problem or to the user's own motives and blind spots. Simple enough to feel almost too basic, and that's usually exactly where it catches something the first answer missed.

### Shifting a team from constant execution into actual reflection
**Blue Work, Red Work** (Leadership Is Language, L. David Marquet): Blue Work is thinking mode — planning, deciding, debating options; Red Work is doing mode — executing a plan with discipline. Most organizations default to constant Red Work and treat thinking as a rare luxury, which is exactly how people end up plowing ahead on a flawed plan. The fix is a deliberate, pre-planned pause to shift into Blue Work and reassess before resuming — the same shape as the existing Weekly Review, applied to a single decision instead of the whole task list.

### Unlocking capability a team already has but isn't using
**Multipliers vs. Diminishers** (Liz Wiseman): a Diminisher hands out solutions, which is faster in the moment but keeps people dependent on the leader; a Multiplier identifies a real opportunity and reframes it as a stretch challenge for the team to solve themselves — often opening with a provocative question like "what would it take to double this?" Research cited in the book found Multiplier leaders access nearly double the intelligence and capability of their teams compared to Diminishers. The instinct to just hand over the answer is usually the Diminisher move, even when it's well-intentioned.

### Communicating from the inside out
**The Golden Circle** (Start with Why, Simon Sinek): Why at the center, How in the middle, What on the outside. Most communication runs outside-in, from What to Why; inspiring communication runs the opposite direction. Complements the existing Just Cause nugget (also Sinek) rather than duplicating it — Just Cause is the decision-filter for strategic choices, the Golden Circle is specifically about how something gets *communicated* once decided.

### How any skill actually develops, including leadership itself
**The four stages of competence** (via The 21 Irrefutable Laws of Leadership, John Maxwell): not knowing what you don't know, realizing you need to learn, consciously acquiring the skill, using it until it feels natural, and finally doing it almost unconsciously out of habit. A useful check on frustration mid-skill — most of the discomfort of learning something new is just being visibly in an early stage, not a sign the skill isn't coming.

### Every strength becomes a weakness at the extreme
**The Dichotomy of Leadership's twelve paired opposites** (Willink/Babin): care about your people, but not so much that the mission fails to get accomplished — and eleven more pairs like it, organized into balancing people, balancing the mission, and balancing oneself. Used as a self-diagnostic: for a specific breakdown, identify which quality is being over-indexed on and consciously pull back toward its opposite, rather than assuming more of a good trait is always better.

### Finding the hidden assumption driving a disagreement
**The Ladder of Inference** (The Fifth Discipline, Peter Senge): the often-invisible steps from observing raw data, to selecting which data gets noticed, adding meaning, making assumptions, drawing conclusions, and finally acting — all happening in a split second. Consciously climbing back down the ladder and questioning the assumption at each rung is what surfaces the hidden belief actually driving a disagreement, instead of two people arguing past each other at the conclusion level.

### The actual foundation under a dysfunctional team
**The Five Dysfunctions pyramid** (Patricia Lencioni): absence of trust, fear of conflict, lack of commitment, avoidance of accountability, inattention to results — stacked, so each one has to be repaired before the level above it can ever be fixed. A team with an accountability problem that's actually a trust problem will keep failing at accountability fixes until trust gets addressed first. A concrete first-meeting exercise for building the foundation: each person answers five prompts aloud (where they grew up, number of siblings, a childhood challenge, their first job, one unique fact nobody else knows) while everyone else just listens — under an hour, and it's specifically designed to build vulnerability-based trust before any conflict work is attempted.

### What to actually look for when hiring or assessing a team player
**Humble, Hungry, Smart** (The Ideal Team Player, Patricia Lencioni): Humble — values team success over personal credit, admits mistakes readily; Hungry — self-motivated, seeks more responsibility without needing to be pushed; Smart — people-smart, not IQ-smart, has common sense about group dynamics and the impact of their own words. Missing even one of the three creates a specific, predictable kind of team dysfunction — useful as a diagnostic in hiring interviews, peer assessments, and coaching conversations alike, not just at the hiring stage.

### Radical over-sharing as a scaling mechanism
**Shared consciousness** (Team of Teams, Stanley McChrystal): a decentralized, fast-moving problem can't be solved by a hierarchy's normal need-to-know information flow — the fix is deliberately over-sharing context that would normally stay restricted, so people across the organization have the same big-picture understanding and decisions can be pushed down to whoever's actually closest to the action.
