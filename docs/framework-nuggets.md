# Framework Nuggets

A pre-curated "best of" from the `plan-do-reflect-www` one-pagers — short, situational frameworks Proteus can offer in a live conversation, distinct from the standing mechanisms in `daily-work-companion.md`.

**Why this exists instead of live corpus search:** Jenn doesn't want Proteus scanning the full one-pager corpus at runtime to "find good nuggets" — that's a token cost paid on every conversation for content that barely changes. Instead, each book gets read once, the genuinely reusable bits get distilled here, and Proteus just recalls from this short list. Grows book by book as more one-pagers get reviewed (see `pdr-practice-coach.md` for the one-pager intake flow) — this file is the running output of that process, not a separate task.

**How to use these:** offer the matching nugget when Jenn describes a live situation it fits — don't recite the whole list, don't force one in where it doesn't apply. One line, applied, beats a citation.

---

### Deciding something hard
**Five questions for a tough decision** (HBR's 10 Must Reads on Managing Yourself, Vol. 2 — Joseph Badaracco): What are the full consequences of each option? What are my core obligations? What will actually work in the world as it is? Who are we? What can I live with? For right-vs-right dilemmas that need judgment, not just analysis.

### Triaging a long list
**ABCDE method** (Eat That Frog!, Brian Tracy): label every task A (serious consequences if not done), B (should do, mild consequences), C (nice to do, no consequences), D (delegate), or E (eliminate) — then work strictly in order, never touching a B until every A is done. A more mechanical alternative to the Focusing Question when a list is long and needs sorting, not narrowing.

### A team's work isn't flowing
**The five thieves of time** (Making Work Visible, Dominica DeGrandis): too much work in progress, unknown dependencies, unplanned work, conflicting priorities, neglected work. Naming which thief is active is the first step to fixing a flow problem — useful when Jenn's describing a team/throughput issue, not a personal one.

### Declining something
**"I don't" vs. "I can't"** (The Art of Saying No, Damon Zahariades): "I can't" invites negotiation because it sounds like an obstacle to solve around; "I don't" states a standing choice. Swap the phrasing in the moment rather than over-explaining.

### "I feel busy but I don't know where the time goes"
**The 168-hour time log** (168 Hours, Laura Vanderkam): for one full week, record everything in half-hour increments, specifically (e.g. "made stir-fry," not "dinner"). At week's end, tally by category against what you assumed. A diagnostic to run occasionally, not a standing habit — most useful right before a quarterly or yearly review if the picture feels fuzzy.

### Frustrated about something outside her control
**Circle of Influence vs. Circle of Concern** (The 7 Habits of Highly Effective People, Stephen Covey): the Circle of Concern holds everything you worry about, most of which you can't control; the smaller Circle of Influence is what you can actually affect. Spending energy in the Concern circle shrinks the Influence circle over time; spending it inside Influence grows it. Useful alongside the strategy/goals-context check in Delegate or Decline — is this actually something to act on, or just something to stop dwelling on. When it's less "outside my control" and more "everything feels like too much at once" (The Happiness Advantage, Shawn Achor): deliberately shrink down to a small circle she can fully master right now, then expand it step by step as confidence builds.

### Starting something new, or a goal that keeps slipping
**WOOP** (Rethinking Positive Thinking, Gabriele Oettingen): name a feasible **W**ish, vividly imagine the best **O**utcome, honestly identify the real internal **O**bstacle in the way, then make a concrete if-then **P**lan for when that obstacle shows up. The obstacle and if-then plan are what make this different from just visualizing success — pure positive fantasizing without them tends to sap the energy needed to actually pursue the goal.

### Reading a tense workplace moment
**SCARF** (Your Brain at Work, David Rock): five things that trigger a threat-or-reward response independent of the actual content of a conversation — **S**tatus, **C**ertainty, **A**utonomy, **R**elatedness, **F**airness. When a reaction seems bigger than the situation warrants, check whether one of these got threatened; often the fix is restoring that specific thing, not re-explaining the original point.

### Reframing a setback
**Perception, Action, Will** (The Obstacle Is the Way, Ryan Holiday): change how you're perceiving the obstacle first (neutral or opportunity-focused, not catastrophic), then take deliberate, persistent action to work around or through it, then apply will to endure and accept whatever genuinely can't be changed. A clean three-step frame when something's gone wrong and the instinct is to spiral rather than move.

### An underperforming or disengaged team member
**Runners, Joggers, Walkers, Riders** (Move Your Bus, Ron Clark): Runners are high-effort top performers, Joggers are steady but lack the confidence to push harder, Walkers contribute minimally and resist change, Riders are dead weight who only perform when watched. Naming which one someone actually is changes what kind of conversation helps — a Jogger needs confidence-building, a Rider needs direct accountability, and treating one as the other usually goes nowhere.

### Digging into a debate or a strongly-held opinion
**Think like a scientist, not a preacher, prosecutor, or politician** (Think Again, Adam Grant): a preacher defends sacred beliefs, a prosecutor attacks the other side's view to win, a politician tells people what they want to hear — a scientist treats their own opinion as a hypothesis, actively looks for disconfirming evidence, and updates when the evidence warrants it. Pairs well with the five-questions-for-a-tough-decision nugget above when the "decision" is really about whether to change her own mind.

### Making a point land — writing or speaking
**"I believe that..." / "So what?" / "Why?"** (Get to the Point!, Brant Pinvidic — from the Communication corpus): a vague topic isn't a point. State it as a defensible assertion starting with "I believe that," then stress-test it — is it consequential enough to matter (so what), and can it actually be defended (why) — until it's sharp enough to organize a message around.

### Building a persuasive case
**Ethos, logos, pathos** (Five Stars, Carmine Gallo — Aristotle's classical framework): credibility, logical argument, emotional connection. A pitch or case that leans on only one of the three usually lands flat — check which of the three is missing before adding more of what's already there.

### Giving feedback that actually lands
**Praise the process, not the trait** (Mindset, Carol Dweck): "the approach you took on X worked because..." is repeatable and improvable; "you're so good at X" reads as a verdict on the person, not something they did. This is the framing check behind `hard-conversations.md`'s feedback step — most useful for performance reviews and 1:1s, where the identity stakes are highest.
